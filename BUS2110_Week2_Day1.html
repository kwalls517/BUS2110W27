Data, information, knowledge

Data becomes information, and information becomes knowledge

Each step adds meaning. A point-of-sale terminal produces data all night. Value appears only when someone makes sense of it.

1. Data
2. Information
3. Knowledge
4. Decision

Data

Raw facts with no context.

Ex: Table 12, 7:42 pm, $86.50

Information

Data organized so it answers a question.

Ex: Saturday sales were $3,100, the best night of the week.

Knowledge

Understanding of why, and what to do.

Ex: Reservations fill weekends, so staff up Friday and Saturday.

Decision

A choice made with that knowledge.

Ex: Schedule two extra servers on Friday and Saturday.

The same fact can be data in one setting and information in another. What matters is whether it has been organized to answer a question.

Organizational information

Information comes from inside and outside the organization

Good decisions use both. The Copper Table's own records tell it what happened. Outside sources tell it what is going on around it.

Internal information

Produced by the organization's own operations.

Examples: sales from the register, payroll hours, inventory counts, staff schedules.

External information

Comes from outside the organization.

Examples: competitor prices, supplier price lists, the weather forecast, online reviews, the city event calendar.

Shared across departments

Moves between functions so each one sees the whole picture.

Examples: kitchen, dining room, and marketing all using the same reservation and sales data.

Decision levels

Different people need different information

Operational staff need detail right now. Managers need summaries. Owners need trends. Decide which level each person works at.

A line cook needs the live list of tickets waiting at the grill.

The cook makes minute-by-minute decisions. Detailed, immediate information fits the operational level.

The general manager reviews last week's sales and labor cost by day.

A weekly summary supports short-term planning and control, which is tactical. (Some textbooks call this level managerial.)

The owner studies three years of sales to decide whether to open a second location.

A multi-year trend supports a long-range direction, which is strategic.

Why visualize

We chart data for three reasons

Record

Keep a clear picture of what happened, so it can be reviewed later.

Copper Table: a monthly sales chart for the owner's file.

Communicate

Help other people see a point quickly.

Copper Table: a chart that shows the staff why weekends need more servers.

Analyze

Discover patterns that a table of numbers hides.

Copper Table: a line that shows sales climbing for six weeks.

Data is useless unless you can make someone care. "Sales were 5% lower in Q3 than Q2" is a sentence. A chart shows when the drop started and lets the reader judge for themselves.
Chart types

Match the chart to the question

Column chart

Compares categories. Vertical bars, one for each category.

Ex: sales for each day of the week.

Bar chart

Compares categories with long names. The same idea, drawn horizontally.

Ex: sales for twelve menu items.

Line chart

Shows change over time. Points joined by a line.

Ex: weekly sales over six weeks.

Pie chart

Shows parts of a whole. Works only for a few slices that add to 100%.

Ex: share of entree sales for four dishes.

Four more charts you will meet

Scatter plot

Best for: Showing whether two numbers move together.

Copper Table: Guests per night against sales. More guests, more sales.

Watch out: A pattern is not proof one thing causes the other.

Area chart

Best for: Showing change over time with emphasis on the amount.

Copper Table: Weekly sales, with the area under the line filled in.

Doughnut chart

Best for: Parts of a whole, like a pie, with room in the center.

Copper Table: How guests paid: card, cash, or gift card.

Combo chart

Best for: Showing two measures that use different units.

Copper Table: Sales as columns and guests as a line, on the same days.

Compare total sales across the seven days of the week.

Long names fit better as horizontal labels: a bar chart.

Chart elements

A readable chart has a few essential parts

Title

Says what the chart shows, in plain words.

Axis titles

Name each axis and give units, such as dollars.

Legend

Explains colors when there is more than one series.

Data labels

Show exact values on the bars, points, or slices.

Gridlines

Light lines that help the eye read values. Keep them faint.
In Excel, select the chart and use the + (Chart Elements) button beside it to add or remove each part.

Good vs. bad

A chart can tell the truth or mislead

Most misleading charts are not lies. They are careless choices that change what the reader sees. Four habits keep a chart honest.

Start at zero

A bar or column chart should start its axis at zero, so the size of the bar matches the size of the number.

Choose the right type

Use the chart that fits the question, such as a line for time and a pie only for a few parts of a whole.

Keep it simple

No 3D, no heavy decoration, and no more slices or colors than the reader can follow.

Label everything

Give the chart a clear title, axis titles with units, and labels the reader can read.

Good vs. bad

What is wrong with each chart?

Each description matches a common mistake. Choose the flaw.

A column chart's vertical axis starts at 11,000, so a 5% drop looks like a collapse.

Cutting off the axis exaggerates small differences, because the bars no longer match the numbers.

A pie chart with 14 slices, most under 2%.

Readers cannot compare many thin slices. A sorted bar chart works better.

A line connects Burger, Pasta, and Salad, as if one dish turns into another.

Lines imply a continuous change. Separate categories need columns or bars.

A chart titled "Chart 1" has no axis titles and no units.

Without a clear title and units, the reader does not know what the numbers mean.

A 3D pie makes the front slice look bigger than an equal slice at the back.

Perspective changes the apparent size of slices. Flat charts are more honest.
