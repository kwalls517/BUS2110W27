Choose

Porter's three generic strategies

Every business has to answer two questions: how will we win customers (lower cost, or something unique), and how wide a market will we serve (broad, or one narrow segment). The answers give three strategies, and focus comes in two variants.

Cost leadership

Be the lowest-cost provider in a broad market, either to price below rivals or to keep average prices and earn a bigger margin.

Example: Walmart streamlines its supply chain to keep costs and prices down.

Differentiation

Offer unique features, quality, or experiences across a broad market that customers will pay a premium for.

Example: Apple's product design; Tesla's technology and brand.

Focus

Concentrate on one narrow segment (a customer group, a region, a product line) and serve it better than anyone. It comes as cost focus or differentiation focus.

Example: A luxury hotel chain for ultra-wealthy guests.

Strategy shows up as a position: price and uniqueness

You can place any business on two measures. Price runs left to right. How unique the offering is runs bottom to top. Where a business sits tells you which strategy it is really following.

Low price, standard offer

Bottom left. This is cost leadership: win on price and efficiency.

High price, unique offer

Top right. This is differentiation: customers pay more because they get something they cannot get elsewhere.

High price, nothing special

Bottom right. This is the danger zone, stuck in the middle or simply overpriced. Customers have no reason to pay.

Focus is about the segment you serve, so it does not get its own spot on this map. A narrow-segment business still lands in the bottom left (cost focus) or top right (differentiation focus). Unique and cheap (top left) is rare and hard to keep, because rivals can copy it.

Analyze

Porter's five forces: what shapes an industry's profit

Before choosing a strategy, a business asks whether the industry itself is attractive. Five forces push on profit. The more of them that are strong, the harder it is to earn a return.

New entrants

How easily can newcomers enter? Low barriers invite competition.

Example: fintech companies entering banking.

Substitutes

Can customers meet the same need a different way?

Example: streaming replacing traditional TV.

Buyer power

Can customers push for lower prices or better quality?

Example: shoppers using price comparison apps.

Supplier power

Can suppliers raise prices or restrict supply?

Example: semiconductor shortages in automotive.

Rivalry

How fiercely do existing competitors fight for the same customers?

Example: Uber versus Lyft.

Read the result

Strong forces mean lower profit potential. Weak forces mean a more attractive industry.

The value chain: the activities that turn inputs into a product

Strategy only works if a business carries it out. Porter's value chain splits a business into the activities it performs. Primary activities create and deliver the product. Support activities help every primary activity. The gap between the value created and the cost of the activities is the margin.

Primary activities

Inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, and service.

Support activities

Firm infrastructure, human resource management, technology development, and procurement.

Why it matters for MIS

The chain shows where a business creates value, so it is a model for deciding which IT projects matter most.

Sustain

An advantage only matters if rivals can't easily copy it

Sustainable advantages come from things like proprietary technology, strong brand loyalty, and economies of scale. Even those can erode with competitor innovation, market shifts, or changing customer preferences. How easily could Lantern Kitchen copy each move? Tap a label.

Dropping prices by 10% for a month.

Easy to copy. A rival with more scale can match a price cut and outlast you.

Years of guest data and a loyalty program that guests actually use.

Hard to copy. Loyalty and data take years to build and can’t be bought.

Adding a trendy item to the menu.

Easy to copy. A rival can add the same item next week.

A strong local reputation built over years.

Hard to copy. Brand loyalty is earned over time.

Buying the same online-ordering app every other restaurant uses.

Easy to copy. Anyone can buy a ready-made product, so it is no edge by itself.

A chain’s ability to negotiate lower supplier costs by buying in huge volume.

Hard to copy. Economies of scale are the kind of advantage Walmart relies on.
