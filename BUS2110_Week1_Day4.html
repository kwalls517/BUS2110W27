Hardware

Four parts do the work inside a computer

Every computer, from a phone to a tablet to a server, is built around the same four parts. Think of a restaurant kitchen.

CPU

Central processing unit. The part that follows a program's instructions and does the calculations. When people say a computer is "fast," they usually mean its CPU.

In the kitchen: the chef doing the cooking.

Primary memory (RAM)

Short-term workspace that holds whatever the computer is working on right now. It is fast, but it empties when the power goes off. Saved files live in storage instead.

In the kitchen: counter space. More counter lets the chef work on more dishes at once.

System board (motherboard)

The main circuit board that every other part plugs into, so they can share power and pass data to each other.

In the kitchen: the building's wiring and plumbing.

Hardware interface (ports)

The connection points where outside devices, called peripherals, plug in: USB, HDMI for a screen, Ethernet for a wired network.

In the kitchen: the pass-through window where orders come in and plates go out.

Hardware

Hardware comes in five types

Hardware is any physical part of a system, anything you could touch. It runs every piece of software a business uses. It comes in five types, grouped by job.

TypeIts jobTerms you'll hear, in plain language
InputGets data into the systemKeyboard and touchscreen. Barcode scanner: reads the striped code on a package. Sensor: measures something automatically, like a freezer thermometer that reports its temperature.
ProcessingDoes the work: follows instructions and calculatesCPU: the main processor from the last slides. GPU (graphics processing unit): a processor built for drawing images on a screen, now also used to run AI.
StorageKeeps data, even when the power is offSSD (solid-state drive): the storage chip inside most laptops and phones. Cloud storage: saving files on another company's computers over the internet, like Google Drive or iCloud.
OutputShows or delivers results to peopleMonitor or screen, printer, speakers.
NetworkingLets devices talk to each otherRouter: the box that connects a building's network to the internet. Switch: connects many wired devices inside one building. Access point: broadcasts the Wi-Fi signal to a room.
Why does a business professional need to know? To choose hardware that controls cost, to decide when to upgrade or replace, and to make sure it works with the business's software.

Software

Software tells the hardware what to do

Hardware is useless without software: the programs that tell it what to do. We sort business software into four types by what it is for. To classify a tool, ask: is it running today's work, helping a manager decide, doing one specialized job, or planning the future?

Operational

For: running the day-to-day work as it happens.

Used by: frontline staff, every shift.

You may know: the Square or Toast register at a coffee shop; Shopify running an online store.

At The Copper Table: taking and tracking tonight's orders.

Managerial

For: helping managers compare, budget, and decide for the weeks ahead.

Used by: managers.

You may know: Excel or Google Sheets comparing a budget with what was spent.

At The Copper Table: comparing this week's labor cost with the budget.

Application

For: one specialized task, whatever the level.

Used by: whoever does that task.

You may know: Photoshop, Canva, Word, QuickBooks.

At The Copper Table: running payroll or editing menu photos.

Strategic

For: long-term planning, often with trends and outside data.

Used by: owners and senior leaders.

You may know: Power BI or Tableau dashboards.

At The Copper Table: studying trends to plan a second location.

Notice the link to Day 2's operational, tactical, and strategic information: operational, managerial, and strategic software support those same three levels of decision. Application software is the odd one out, because it is defined by a specialized task, not a level.

Software is not optional, it is strategic. It connects people to the organization's technology and links business processes to technical solutions.

Software

Software can be built for you or bought ready-made

Custom software

Proprietary applications developed by in-house staff and owned entirely by the organization. Example: Uber's app.

Strengths: competitive advantage, a fit for unique requirements, stronger ROI over time, lower total cost of ownership.

Trade-off: costs more time and money.

Ready-made software (COTS)

Commercial off-the-shelf applications developed by a third party and purchased for use. Example: Canva for social media posts.

Strengths: fast to deploy, plug and play, scalable, 24/7 support, upgrades included.

Trade-off: may not fit your needs perfectly and gives no competitive advantage.

Connect it to Day 3: custom software can create an advantage rivals cannot easily copy. Ready-made software is available to every competitor too.

Develop

Two common ways to run a project

Waterfall

Sequential phases: planning, analysis, design, development, testing, implementation, maintenance. Best for well-defined projects with clear requirements.

Strengths: simple, and each phase has defined deliverables.

Weaknesses: time-consuming, poor for short projects, and cannot handle changing requirements.

Example you know: building a house. The blueprint is approved before the foundation is poured, and moving a wall later is expensive. In software: a payroll system that must meet a new tax law by a fixed date.

Agile

An iterative process with continuous collaboration, built in short cycles called sprints of two to four weeks. Best for dynamic projects where requirements may change.

Strengths: flexible, features are easy to add, and users review after every sprint.

Weaknesses: little documentation, needs skilled people, and risks scope creep and budget creep.

Example you know: the apps on your phone. Spotify and Instagram ship small updates every few weeks and keep or drop features based on how people use them.

There is no perfect method. You have to examine your needs. Other methods exist, such as the V-shaped, prototype, spiral, and iterative incremental models.

Develop

Three traps that sink systems projects

Projects rarely fail because the technology is impossible. They fail because of how the project is run. Watch for these three.

Scope creep

The scope is the agreed list of what the project will deliver. Scope creep is when that list keeps growing after work starts, "just one more feature," without adding time or money.

Example: the kitchen-screen project grows to also handle inventory, then staff scheduling, then a loyalty program, and never launches.

Prevent it: write the scope down, and treat every new request as a decision about time and cost.

Poor estimates

Guessing too low on how much time, money, or people the work will take, usually from optimism or from skipping the details.

Example: "Three months" becomes fourteen, and the budget runs out in month six.

Prevent it: break the work into small pieces, estimate each one, compare with past projects, and build in a buffer.

Poor communication

The people who use the system, the people who build it, and the people who pay for it don't share what they know.

Example: nobody tells the developers the kitchen screens must work with greasy gloves on.

Prevent it: involve end users early, and hold regular check-ins, like agile's review after each sprint.

Excel: Chapter 2

Functions do the arithmetic for you

A function is a built-in formula. It starts with =, then the function name, then a range in parentheses. The range C2:C8 means every cell from C2 to C8. Here are five for The Copper Table's weekly sales in C2:C8.

SUM

=SUM(C2:C8)
Adds the numbers. Total sales for the week.

AVERAGE

=AVERAGE(C2:C8)
The mean. Typical sales per night.

MIN

=MIN(C2:C8)
The smallest value. The slowest night.

MAX

=MAX(C2:C8)
The largest value. The busiest night.

COUNT

=COUNT(C2:C8)
How many cells hold numbers. Nights with sales.
Shortcut: AutoSum on the Home tab inserts SUM for you. Always check that the range it highlights is the one you meant.

What the $ does. In a cell reference, the letter is the column and the number is the row. A dollar sign in front of either one locks it, so it doesn't change when you copy the formula.

B5 nothing locked: the reference shifts as you copy.
$B$5 column and row locked: it always means B5.

You may also see $B5 or B$5, which lock just one part. You'll meet those later in Chapter 2.

Think of it this way. A relative reference is like directions: "three rows up from here." Copy directions to a new spot and they lead somewhere else. An absolute reference is like a street address: it leads to the same house no matter where you start.

How to add it: type the $ signs yourself, or click in the reference while editing and press F4 (Windows) or Cmd+T (Mac).

When to use it: whenever every row should point at the same cell, like a total or a tax rate.
