After the practice exam · How it works

A language model predicts the next word

Generative AI creates new content, such as text, images, or code, from patterns learned in training data. A large language model (LLM) works with text. Three ideas explain it, and they fit a kitchen.

Training data

The cookbook. Huge collections of text the model studied. If the data is biased or incomplete, the output can be too.

Neural network

The kitchen team. Layers of simple units that find patterns and turn input into output.

Transformer

The head chef. It uses attention to decide which parts of the input matter most for the next step.
The model does not look facts up. It produces the words that are most likely to come next, based on patterns. Sometimes the likely words are not true.

Hallucinations

When the AI sounds sure and is wrong

A hallucination is output that is convincing but factually wrong or entirely made up. It happens because the model predicts likely words. It can happen when the model goes past its training data, lacks context, or is trained on its own generated text.

Invented sources

Studies, quotes, and links that do not exist.

Invented facts

Dates, numbers, and details about people or companies.

Confident tone

Wrong answers read just as smoothly as right ones.
In high-stakes fields like healthcare or law, a hallucination can do real harm. The more it matters, the more you must check.

Bias

AI can repeat and amplify unfairness

Models learn from large datasets that may include biased or unrepresentative information. The results can reinforce stereotypes or leave some groups out.

Biased training data

Past decisions that favored one group get copied.

Missing groups

People who are rare in the data get worse results.

Stereotypes

Outputs repeat common but unfair assumptions.

Overreliance

People accept the result without thinking.
Bias

Name the source of the problem

A resume screener was trained on past hires, who were mostly from one group, and keeps favoring similar candidates.

The model copied the pattern in its training data. That is biased training data.

A chatbot trained mostly on English gives poor answers to Spanish-speaking guests.

Spanish speakers are underrepresented in the data, so the results are worse for them.

An image tool shows only men when asked for pictures of chefs.

The output repeats a stereotype from its training data.

A manager picks promotions straight from an AI list without reviewing them.

Accepting the output without thinking is overreliance.

Verification

Trust, but check

Verification is how you decide how much to trust an AI answer. The right check depends on what kind of claim it is.

1. Read critically
2. Find the source
3. Cross-check
4. Test with data

Facts and numbers

Find the original source, not just another page repeating it.

Quotes and citations

Search for them. If you cannot find them, assume they may be invented.

Formulas and code

Run them on a small example where you already know the right answer.
Verification

Choose the best check

AI says 62% of restaurants raised prices last year.

A statistic should be traced to its original source before you use it.

AI cites a journal article by an author you have never heard of.

Search for the citation. If it does not exist, the AI invented it.

AI writes an Excel formula for average sales.

Test it on a small example where you know the answer.

AI gives advice on a food-allergy law.

Legal and safety questions need an expert or an official source.

If time allows · Responsible AI

How should AI be used here?

Ask AI to suggest ways to organize your study notes.

Low-risk help with organizing your own work is fine.

Use a statistic that AI gave you in a report.

A number from AI must be checked against its original source before you use it.

Run an AI-written formula on your real data.

Test the formula on a small example with a known answer first.

Paste guests' names and emails into a public AI tool.

This exposes personal data. Do not do it.

Submit AI output you cannot explain or defend.

If you cannot explain it, you do not understand it, and you cannot be accountable for it.

Decide

The AI recommends three promotions. What now?

The Copper Table tries an AI tool that suggests which staff to promote. All three recommended people come from the same group, though the applicant pool was mixed. What should the owner do?

The pattern is a signal to look closer. Measuring rates by group, asking about the training data, and keeping a human accountable for the decision is how bias is caught. Dropping AI entirely or trusting its own explanation does not address the problem.
