Threats

Five common ways attackers get in

Phishing

Fake emails, sites, or calls that trick people into giving up data or money.

Malware

Software built to damage systems or gain access to them.

Ransomware

Malware that locks files until a ransom is paid.

Social engineering

Manipulating people into revealing confidential information.

Tampering

Physically altering devices to steal money, data, or identities.
Other tricks include fake Wi-Fi hotspots, stolen cookies, misleading file names, and malicious ads. No set of products makes an organization fully safe. The goal is to be a harder target than the next one.
Threats

Name the threat

An email says the payroll account is locked and asks the manager to click a link to sign in.

A fake message designed to steal sign-in details is phishing.

Files on the office computer are locked, and a note demands payment to unlock them.

Locking files until a ransom is paid is ransomware.

A caller claims to be from the card company and asks the manager to read out a security code.

Talking a person into revealing information is social engineering.

A small extra device is found attached to the card reader at the register.

Physically altering a device to steal data is tampering.

A free menu-maker download secretly installs a program that records keystrokes.

Software designed to gain access to a system is malware.

Inspect this email

The manager received this message. Select the details that look suspicious, then check. Some parts are fine.

From: Copper Table Payroll <payroll@copper-table-secure.example>
Subject: URGENT: Your account will be closed in 24 hours
Dear user,
We noticed a problem with this month's payroll run. Payroll for the week of January 4 is ready for review. To avoid a delay, click here to verify your sign-in: http://coppertable-login.example/verify and confirm your password.
Attachment: PayrollDetails.zip
Thank you, Payroll Department
Red flags: an unusual sender domain, urgent and threatening language, a generic greeting, a link that goes to an unfamiliar site and asks for a password, and an unexpected zip attachment. The rest is ordinary wording. When unsure, do not click. Contact the sender through a channel you already trust.
Defenses

No single defense is enough, so use layers

People

Training staff to spot phishing and social engineering.

Access control

Strong passwords, multi-factor sign-in, and access only for those who need it.

Software

Keeping systems updated so known holes are closed.

Backups

Copies kept safely apart so a locked system can be restored.

EMV and tokenization

Chip cards and replacing card numbers with tokens that are worthless if stolen.

Cyber insurance

Helps cover costs like recovery, legal fees, and public relations after an incident.
Defenses

Match the defense to the problem

Ransomware locked every file on the office computer. What best limits the damage?

Clean backups stored apart from the system let you restore without paying.

Staff keep clicking links in fake emails. What helps most?

Phishing works on people, so training is the key defense.

The restaurant does not want real card numbers stored in its own systems. What helps?

Tokenization replaces card numbers with tokens that have no value if stolen.

A vendor needs to fix the kitchen printers but has access to the whole network. What fits?

Limit third-party access to only what the job needs.

Even with defenses, a breach could cost more than the restaurant can pay. What helps?

Cyber insurance acts as a financial buffer after an incident.

Case study

How a heating contractor's network access led to 40 million stolen cards

In 2013, malicious software was planted on Target's point-of-sale systems. The way in was a third-party vendor whose access to the network was not properly limited.

1. Vendor given broad access
2. Malware reaches the registers
3. 40 million cards and 70 million customer records exposed

Total expenses

$61 million in that quarter, from the investigation, credit monitoring, call center staffing, and legal costs.

Insurance offset

$44 million expected back from insurance.

Net expense

$17 million, which is $61 million minus $44 million.
The breach was not only a technology failure. A vendor's access was left too wide. Limiting what outsiders can reach is one of the most valuable controls.
Privacy

Security protects data. Privacy governs how it is used.

Security

Keeping data safe from unauthorized access.

Ex: locking the guest database with strong sign-in.

Privacy

Rules about what organizations may collect, store, and do with personal data.

Ex: a guest asks to have their data deleted.

Two early laws set the pattern. The GDPR covers organizations that handle personal data of people in the EU, and the CCPA gives California residents data privacy rights. Both cover any data that relates to an identifiable person, including names, email addresses, and IP addresses. Breaking these rules can bring fines or the loss of the right to do business with the people covered.

Privacy

Which right is being used?

A guest asks the restaurant to delete everything it has stored about them.

The right to be forgotten lets people request deletion of their personal data.

A guest asks to see what data the restaurant holds about them.

The right of access lets people view the data an organization holds.

A guest asks the restaurant to fix a wrong email address in the system.

The right to rectification lets people correct inaccurate data.

A guest asks the restaurant to stop using their data for marketing.

The right to object lets people stop processing for purposes like marketing.

A guest asks for a copy of their data to move to another service.

The right to data portability lets people transfer their data.

Business ethics

Questions that test a decision

Business ethics scholar Laura Nash offered a list of questions to ask before acting. A few of them, in our own words:

Define the problem

Have you described it accurately? How would it look from the other side?

Think about people

Who could this decision hurt? Can you talk with those affected first?

Test the decision

Could you explain it without qualms to your boss, your family, or the public? Would it still look right years from now?
Try them on the Denver case: Is the goal good? Could the method harm someone? Could the department explain it openly?
AI and data

What you type into an AI tool may not stay private

AI tools are useful for working with data, and they are also a new way for data to leak. Once information is pasted into a tool, you may not control where it goes. Treat a prompt as a message to an outside party until you know the tool's rules.

Check the tool

Use the tools your school or employer approves, and learn what they say about storing your data.

Share only what is needed

Ask your question with the minimum data. Often a made-up example works just as well.

Mask first

Remove names, contact details, and ID numbers before you paste anything.
