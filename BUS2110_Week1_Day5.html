Networks

The internet and the web are not the same thing

A network is a collection of computers connected through a communication link to share hardware, data, and applications.

The internet

A global network of interconnected computers and devices. Its purpose is to share information, resources, and services.

The web

A service that runs on the internet. It includes websites and web pages that you reach through a browser such as Chrome or Safari.

How a web page reaches you:

1. Connection
2. Requesting data
3. Data exchange
Your device connects to the internet through an ISP (internet service provider) via cables, satellites, or cellular networks.
You search for a website. Your browser asks a DNS (Domain Name System) to find the right server's address.
The server's data is broken into packets, sent back to your device, reassembled, and displayed.

Networks come in sizes and are built from five components

LAN (local area network)

Computers connected within a single location or organization.

Examples: office computers sharing a printer; a home Wi-Fi network. At The Copper Table: the tablets, kitchen screens, and printer.

WAN (wide area network)

Computers connected over a wide geographic area using communication lines from an outside provider.

Examples: a multinational company linking branch offices; a university campus network. At The Copper Table: the link out to the cloud service.

Router

Connects multiple networks together.

Network switch

Connects computing devices to the network through cabling.

Ethernet cable

Physical cable connecting a device to a network.

Fiber optic cable

Glass-based cable for long-distance connections.

Access point

Connects devices to a network wirelessly.

Cloud

The cloud means renting computing over the internet

Without the internet there is no cloud. Organizations rent services instead of owning them, by signing up for a subscription (some are free). Nothing is installed on local hardware.

On-demand

Access resources anytime without manual intervention.

Scalable

Scale resources up or down based on demand.

Pay-as-you-go

Pay only for the resources you use.

Resource pooling

Share resources among many users securely and efficiently.

Accessible

Reach services from anywhere with an internet connection.

Mobility

Mobile devices put the business in everyone's pocket

Smartphones and tablets, connected by Wi-Fi or cellular networks and backed by the cloud, let people work, sell, and serve from anywhere. It is broad network access in action.

What it makes possible

  • Servers take orders and payments tableside.
  • Guests order and pay from their phones.
  • The owner checks sales from home.
  • Staff get schedule changes instantly.

What it puts at risk

  • A lost or stolen device holds business data.
  • Public Wi-Fi can expose information.
  • Personal phones used for work are harder to control.
  • No signal means no service.

Excel: Chapter 2

The IF function makes the spreadsheet decide

SUM and AVERAGE report numbers. IF makes a decision. It tests a condition and returns one result if it is true and another if it is false.

=IF(test, value if true, value if false)

Example: =IF(C2>=1800,"Busy","Quiet") labels a night "Busy" when its sales are at least $1,800.

>

greater than

<

less than

>=

greater than or equal to

<=

less than or equal to

=

equal to
Two rules: put any text result in quotation marks, and compare against a locked cell, like $C$10, so the formula still works when you fill it down.

Excel: Chapter 2

Three questions about IF

1. What does =IF(C2>=1800,"Busy","Quiet") return when C2 is 1700?

1700 is not at least 1800, so the test is false and IF returns the third argument, "Quiet".

2. Why write $C$10 instead of C10 in the IF formula?

Without dollar signs the reference would slide down to C11, C12, and so on.

3. Which formula counts the cells in E2:E8 that say Busy?

COUNTIF counts the cells that meet a condition. SUM adds numbers, and IF works on one test at a time.

Analytics

Analytics answers three kinds of questions

Business analytics uses quantitative methods to turn data into informed decisions. Think of a doctor's visit: describe the symptoms, predict the illness, prescribe the treatment.

Descriptive

What happened? Describes the past status of the domain using techniques such as reporting or data visualization.

Copper Table: Which night had the most sales?

Predictive

What will happen? Applies statistical and computational methods and models to past and current data to predict the future.

Copper Table: How many covers will we have next Saturday?

Prescriptive

What should we do? Uses predictive results with optimization and simulation to recommend actions that lead to a desired outcome.

Copper Table: How many servers should we schedule?

This course starts with descriptive analytics. Everything you did today with SUM, AVERAGE, MAX, MIN, and IF is descriptive.
