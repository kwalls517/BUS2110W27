The internet and the web are not the same thing

A network is a collection of computers connected through a communication link to share hardware, data, and applications.

The internet

A global network of interconnected computers and devices. Its purpose is to share information, resources, and services.

The web

A service that runs on the internet. It includes websites and web pages that you reach through a browser such as Chrome or Safari.

Networks

Networks come in two sizes: LAN and WAN

A network is two or more devices connected so they can share data, hardware like a printer, and applications. The difference between a LAN and a WAN is distance, and who owns the connections.

LAN (local area network)

Devices connected inside one location: a home, an office, a restaurant. The business owns and runs the equipment.

You know it: your home Wi-Fi; the network in a campus building. At The Copper Table: the tablets, kitchen screen, printer, and register.

WAN (wide area network)

Networks connected across long distances: between cities, states, or countries, over lines rented from an outside provider.

You know it: a bank linking all its branches; a chain store's registers reporting to headquarters. The internet itself is the biggest WAN. At The Copper Table: the link to the cloud ordering system, or to a second location.

Networks

Five pieces of equipment build every network

You've probably seen most of these without knowing their names.

Router

Connects different networks together, like your home network to the internet, and decides where each piece of data goes next.

You've seen it: the box your internet company installs, often with antennas.

Network switch

Connects many wired devices inside one network and passes data only to the device it's meant for.

You've seen it: a box full of cable ports in an office closet.

Ethernet cable

The physical cable that plugs one device into the network. Wired is steadier and faster than Wi-Fi.

You've seen it: the cable that plugs into a desktop or game console.

Fiber optic cable

Glass strands that carry data as pulses of light, fast and over long distances.

You've seen it: "fiber internet" ads; the undersea cables linking continents.

Access point

Broadcasts a wireless signal so phones, laptops, and tablets can join the network without cables.

You've seen it: the white disks on classroom ceilings.

Cloud

The cloud means renting computing over the internet

"The cloud" is a nickname for computers in someone else's data center that you use over the internet. When you stream a show on Netflix, back up photos to iCloud, or open a Google Doc, the files and programs aren't really on your device. They live on servers run by companies like Amazon, Google, and Microsoft.

For a business, the cloud means renting instead of owning: signing up for a service, often a monthly subscription, instead of buying servers and hiring people to run them. Think of the difference between owning a car and calling an Uber. Without the internet, there is no cloud. Five features define it:

On-demand

Get more computing yourself, anytime, without calling anyone.

e.g. upgrading your iCloud storage in two taps.

Scalable

Grows or shrinks with demand.

e.g. a ticket site handling a concert-sale rush, then shrinking back.

Pay-as-you-go

Pay only for what you use, like an electric bill.

e.g. paying per order processed, not for an idle server.

Resource pooling

Many customers share the same data centers, safely kept apart.

e.g. millions of Google Drive users on shared servers.

Accessible

Reach it from any device with an internet connection.

e.g. opening Canvas from your phone or a library computer.

Mobility

Mobile devices put the business in everyone's pocket

Smartphones and tablets, connected by Wi-Fi or cellular networks and backed by the cloud, let people work, sell, and serve from anywhere. It is broad network access in action.

What it makes possible

  • Servers take orders and payments tableside.
  • Guests order and pay from their phones.
  • The owner checks sales from home.
  • Staff get schedule changes instantly.

What it puts at risk

  • A lost or stolen device holds business data.
  • Public Wi-Fi can expose information.
  • Personal phones used for work are harder to control.
  • No signal means no service.

The IF function makes the spreadsheet decide

SUM and AVERAGE report numbers. IF makes a decision. It tests a condition and returns one result if it is true and another if it is false.

=IF(test, value if true, value if false)

Example: =IF(C2>=1800,"Busy","Quiet") labels a night "Busy" when its sales are at least $1,800.

>

greater than

<

less than

>=

greater than or equal to

<=

=

equal to
Two rules: put any text result in quotation marks, and compare against a locked cell, like $C$10, so the formula still works when you fill it down.

Excel: Chapter 2

Three questions about IF

1. What does =IF(C2>=1800,"Busy","Quiet") return when C2 is 1700?

1700 is not at least 1800, so the test is false and IF returns the third argument, "Quiet".

2. Why write $C$10 instead of C10 in the IF formula?

Without dollar signs the reference would slide down to C11, C12, and so on.

3. Which formula counts the cells in E2:E8 that say Busy?

COUNTIF counts the cells that meet a condition. SUM adds numbers, and IF works on one test at a time.

Analytics

Analytics answers three kinds of questions

Business analytics uses quantitative methods to turn data into informed decisions. Think of a doctor's visit: describe the symptoms, predict the illness, prescribe the treatment.

Descriptive

What happened? Describes the past status of the domain using techniques such as reporting or data visualization.

Copper Table: Which night had the most sales?

Predictive

What will happen? Applies statistical and computational methods and models to past and current data to predict the future.

Copper Table: How many covers will we have next Saturday?

Prescriptive

What should we do? Uses predictive results with optimization and simulation to recommend actions that lead to a desired outcome.

Copper Table: How many servers should we schedule?

This course starts with descriptive analytics. Everything you did today with SUM, AVERAGE, MAX, MIN, and IF is descriptive.
