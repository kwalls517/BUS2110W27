Digital business

Digital business is bigger than an online store

These terms overlap, but they are not the same. Each one is a wider circle than the one before it.

E-commerce

Buying and selling goods or services over the internet.

Copper Table: a guest orders dinner and pays on the website.

Social commerce

Selling or promoting through social media platforms and the communities on them.

Copper Table: a guest orders through a link on the restaurant's Instagram page.

Digital business

Using digital technology to run operations, reach customers, and create value, whether or not anything is sold online.

Copper Table: online ordering, kitchen screens, reservations, analytics, and staff scheduling working together.

E-commerce is one part of digital business. A restaurant that only takes phone orders can still run a digital business through its kitchen systems and data.
Digital business

Who is selling to whom?

Online business is often described by the two parties in the transaction.

B2C

Business to consumer. A company sells to individual customers.

Ex: The Copper Table sells dinners and gift cards to guests.

B2B

Business to business. A company sells to another company.

Ex: A produce wholesaler sells to the restaurant.

C2C

Consumer to consumer. Individuals sell to each other, usually through a platform.

Ex: Two guests trade a gift card on a marketplace site.

Customer journey

Online guests drop out at every step

A funnel follows people through the steps toward an order. Fewer people make it through each step, and the biggest drop shows where to focus.

1. Visit
2. Start an order
3. Reach checkout
4. Pay
Each step has its own rate, the share of people who continue. Improving the weakest step usually adds more orders than polishing a step that already works.

Digital metrics

Four numbers tell you how a campaign performed

Impressions

How many times the content was shown.

Ex: an Instagram post shown 20,000 times.

Click rate

Clicks divided by impressions.

Ex: 600 clicks on 20,000 impressions is 3%.

Order rate

Orders divided by clicks.

Ex: 48 orders from 600 clicks is 8%.

Cost per order

Money spent divided by orders.

Ex: $240 spent for 48 orders is $5.00 each.

Each metric answers a different question: was it seen, was it interesting, did it work, and was it worth the cost?

Social media

Social media runs on three roles

A social media information system (SMIS) supports sharing content among a network of users. Three roles make it work.

User communities

Online communities of people who share information.

Ex: local diners swapping restaurant photos in a group.

Social media sponsors

Companies and organizations that choose to have a presence on a platform.

Ex: The Copper Table's business page.

Application providers

Companies that operate the social media sites.

Ex: the company that runs the platform and sells the ads.

Social media

Two kinds of communities matter most to businesses

Defenders of Belief

Groups that share a common belief and form around it.

Ex: supporters of local, farm-to-table food.

Seekers of Truth

Groups with a shared desire to learn something, solve a problem, or make something happen.

Ex: home cooks working out why a sauce keeps splitting.

By paying attention to these groups, a business can build loyalty among existing customers and reach new ones whose interests match what it offers.
Social media

Defender of Belief or Seeker of Truth?

A group that champions locally sourced food and shares restaurants that match.

The group is organized around a belief, so it is a Defender of Belief.

A forum where people ask how to cook for guests with food allergies.

The group is organized around solving a problem, so it is a Seeker of Truth.

A thread where suppliers and chefs work out how to fix a late delivery.

Solving a specific problem together marks a Seeker of Truth.

Followers who loyally defend a favorite restaurant against a bad review.

A shared belief in the place makes this a Defender of Belief.

Interpreting evidence

A number is not a conclusion

Online data arrives fast and looks precise. Before acting on it, ask four questions.

Is it only a correlation?

Two things moving together does not mean one caused the other.

Is the sample big enough?

A few results can swing wildly by chance.

Is the comparison fair?

Holiday weeks, promotions, and weather can distort a comparison.

What is missing?

A number without its cost, context, or denominator can mislead.
Interpreting evidence

What is wrong with the claim?

Name the weakness in each argument.

Posts with an emoji got more likes, so adding emojis will raise our sales.

Likes and emojis appear together, but that does not show emojis caused sales. This is correlation, not causation.

Our new post got 3 orders in one day, so it is our best campaign.

Three orders is too few to judge. A tiny sample can be luck.

Sales rose 20% from November to December, so our new menu is working.

December is the holiday season. A fair comparison needs the same month a year earlier.

Instagram brought the most orders, so it is our most efficient channel.

Orders ignore cost. Efficiency needs spend, such as cost per order.

Where should the $300 ad budget go?

The owner can spend $300 this month. Email costs $0.50 per order but reaches only the 4,000 people on the list. Instagram costs $5.00 per order and reaches many more people. Which plan fits the evidence best?

Cost per order favors Email, but the list is limited. Using the efficient channel first and testing the others with the remainder follows the evidence without treating a small test as proof.
